# ADR 012: Share immutable closed goals between monthly transitions

The 30-year Earth diagnostic retained 142 MiB of person state. One person had 287 goals, mostly closed history; cloning every completed goal and its evidence arrays each month dominated transition allocation alongside the social graph.

Monthly transitions now deep-copy active goals and share closed goal records with the input, following the existing treatment of deceased people. Closed goals remain immutable; all goal mutators were checked to operate only on active goals, except initial creation of a new person. Nothing is deleted, no event is added, and canonical JSON/state outcomes remain unchanged. Regression tests freeze nested closed-goal evidence and compare real future events/resources/current state against an otherwise identical person without the extra history.

Alternatives were deleting old goals, moving them into a new archive format, or increasing the process heap. Sharing preserves all historical records and existing persistence compatibility without those behavior or deployment changes. The social graph and persisted historical arrays still grow; this is an allocation improvement, not a claim of fixed total memory usage. Long-run artifacts record the source version actually loaded, including runs started before this optimization.

A final ownership audit found that succession clears an incumbent title after death. Deceased people referenced by a current office or pending power record are therefore also copied before a transition. Other deceased records remain shared. This preserves the returned world/event values while preventing a caller’s previous state from changing and making a title change disappear from its journal delta. A frozen post-death snapshot is advanced through a real succession and recovered through a JSON-encoded patch in regression coverage. The long-run artifacts loaded the earlier allocation strategy; the final journal ownership fix has dedicated regression coverage and does not change those simulated outcomes.
